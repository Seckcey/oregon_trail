# The Oregon Trail: A History

*Research compiled 2026-08-24 (web-sourced; URLs inline).*

## Origins (1971)

The Oregon Trail began as a classroom improvisation. In December 1971, Don Rawitsch, a Carleton College senior student-teaching an eighth-grade history unit in Minneapolis, planned a board game simulating a wagon journey; his roommates Bill Heinemann and Paul Dillenberger — math students with programming experience — suggested a computer program instead ([The74Million](https://www.the74million.org/article/oregon-trail-at-50-how-three-teachers-created-the-computer-game-that-inspired-and-diverted-generations-of-students/); [Slate](https://slate.com/news-and-politics/2021/11/oregon-trail-game-history-inventor-don-rawitsch.html)).

Written in BASIC over ~2 weeks for a teletype terminal on a mainframe timesharing system, it debuted in Rawitsch's classroom December 3, 1971. Students typed decisions — food/ammo purchases, hunting, ford vs. caulk — and the game (probabilities loosely drawn from pioneer diaries) determined the party's fate. The original was deleted from the shared computer after ~2 weeks and thought lost for a time ([Wikipedia — 1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oregon_Trail_(1971_video_game)); [Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/innovation/how-you-wound-playing-em-oregon-trailem-computer-class-180959851/)).

## MECC and the road to Apple II (1974–1985)

In 1974 Rawitsch joined the newly formed Minnesota Educational Computing Consortium (MECC, created by the Minnesota legislature in 1973) and brought the code with him. MECC rebuilt the game in 1975, calibrating event probabilities from pioneer diaries, and distributed it on the statewide timesharing network. First published source: *Creative Computing*, May–June 1978 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/oregon-trail-computer-game)).

In 1978 MECC partnered with the fledgling Apple Computer and ported the game to the Apple II (adapted by John Cook) — a partnership that helped Apple dominate K–12 for two decades ([Museum of Play](https://www.museumofplay.org/blog/mecc-the-company-that-launched-educational-gaming/)). MECC began selling nationally in 1981; by the mid-80s the game was a fixture in US schools.

## The classic 1985 Apple IIe edition

The canonical version — the one Gen X/millennial players remember — shipped in 1985 after ~10 months of work led by designer R. Philip Bouchard and programmer John Krenz, exploiting the Apple IIe's speed and color graphics. Hunting and river crossings became interactive mini-games ([Philip Bouchard](https://www.philipbouchard.com/oregon-trail/early-versions.html); [Wikipedia — 1985](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oregon_Trail_(1985_video_game))). Bundled into MECC's school packages, it became the source of the iconic "You have died of dysentery" screen.

## Later editions and corporate fate

- **Oregon Trail Deluxe** (1992 DOS/Mac; 1993 Windows).
- **Oregon Trail II** (1995, under SoftKey) — multiple starting points and routes.
- **3rd Edition** (1997), **4th Edition** (1999), **5th Edition** (2001) under The Learning Company/Riverdeep.

MECC was privatized in 1991, went public 1994, acquired by SoftKey 1996 (SoftKey rebranded as The Learning Company); the MECC name was retired ~1999. The Learning Company passed through Mattel, Gores, Riverdeep, and eventually **Houghton Mifflin Harcourt**, retaining and actively defending the Oregon Trail trademark (sued Zynga over *FrontierVille* in 2011) ([Wikipedia — MECC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MECC); [IP Brief](http://www.ipbrief.net/2011/05/24/the-oregon-trail-v-frontierville-a-trademark-battle-for-our-childhood/)).

**Ownership today:** The Learning Company (HMH subsidiary) holds the trademark and legacy titles; **Gameloft** holds copyright to its 2021/2022 reimagining, developed under license ([Wikipedia — 2021](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Oregon_Trail_(2021_video_game))).

## The 2021 Gameloft / Apple Arcade remake

Modern visuals over the classic resource/party-management core. Standout: authentic representation — three Indigenous historians/consultants (incl. Margaret Huettl, U. Nebraska) advised on naming, speech, music, material culture; first-ever playable Native American characters and story content, plus Black pioneer narratives ([PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/bringing-native-american-and-black-stories-to-apple-arcades-the-oregon-trail/); [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/the-oregon-trail-on-apple-arcade-addresses-native-american-stereotypes/1100-6491409/)). Reception largely positive (visual polish, choice weight, improved representation; some found the loop repetitive). Joint winner, Best Storytelling, 2022 Mobile Games Awards.

## Cultural legacy

- "You have died of dysentery" ranks among gaming's most famous lines ([Know Your Meme](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/you-have-died-of-dysentery)).
- "The Oregon Trail generation" = the Gen X/millennial cusp cohort who played it in school.
- By 1989, roughly **1 in 3 US school districts** had it installed; in 1995 it generated about a third of MECC's $30M annual revenue ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90702587/oregon-trail-computer-game-50th-anniversary)).
- **65+ million copies** sold across editions ([Oregon Business](https://oregonbusiness.com/5910-long-journey-for-oregon-trail-game/)).
- World Video Game Hall of Fame inductee (2016); passed its 50th anniversary in 2021.
